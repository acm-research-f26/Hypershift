# A6 — Literature comparison (verified citations only)

**How these were verified.** Each paper below was checked by web search on 2026-10-22 for authors, venue, year and the abstract-level finding. The figures quoted come from abstracts or bibliographic records. Where only secondary summaries were available, the entry says so. No paper's full text was read.

**Scope of what these papers can and cannot do here.** They define what has been shown elsewhere and which experiments are worth running. None of them validates a HyperShift result: the universes, periods, data and targets all differ.

| Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), *Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning*, RFS 33(5):2223–2273 | ML methods (linear, penalised, PCR/PLS, GBRT, RF, neural nets) for predicting individual-stock returns | All CRSP US stocks; about 94 firm characteristics plus macro interactions; **monthly** horizon; decades of history | Trees and neural nets beat linear models. The gain comes from nonlinear interactions. The dominant predictors are momentum, liquidity and volatility variants. | **Partly.** FB compared Ridge, LightGBM and an MLP on 20 price/volume characteristics at 1–20-day horizons, on 462 current S&P 500 survivors. | No accounting characteristics. Large caps only, where documented ML gains are smaller (see Avramov et al.). Daily horizon. Five test years. Survivor universe. | FB's null for LightGBM vs Ridge (+0.0036, CI ±0.013) is **not** a test of GKX. The only like-for-like test needs point-in-time firm characteristics at a monthly horizon. |
| Avramov, Cheng & Metzker (2023), *Machine Learning vs. Economic Restrictions*, Management Science 69(5) (DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.4449) | Whether ML return predictability survives economic restrictions | US stocks; ML signals from the GKX-type literature | Profits come mainly from **hard-to-arbitrage stocks** (microcaps, distressed firms) and high limits-to-arbitrage periods. Excluding them sharply cuts profits, and costs cut them further (secondary summary: −62% from excluding microcaps). | **Indirectly.** HyperShift's universe is large-cap S&P 500 only, which is exactly where this paper says ML gains are weakest. | Their setup is monthly with broad characteristics. | Explains why a large-cap price-only universe is a hard place to find ML gains. Argues against "add more model" and for a different universe or data if the goal is to find predictability. |
| Krauss, Do & Huck (2017), *Deep neural networks, gradient-boosted trees, random forests: Statistical arbitrage on the S&P 500*, EJOR 259(2):689–702 | DNN/GBT/RF ensembles on lagged returns, daily long-short top/bottom k | S&P 500 constituents **with survivor bias removed**, 1992–2015; one-day-ahead | Ensemble returns above 0.45% per day before costs (k = 10), with profits declining in recent years (abstract). | **Closest analogue.** FB's lag blocks, ridge:lags and lgbm:rich are daily S&P 500 lagged-return learners. | FB evaluates 2019–2023 only, which falls in the post-decay era the abstract mentions. FB uses factor-neutral RankIC and 5-day holds; Krauss et al. use 1-day long-short with no neutralisation. FB has survivorship; Krauss et al. do not. | FB's tiny ICs are consistent with the decay that paper reports. A survivor-free S&P 500 replication of a 1-day reversal-learner is the cheap external benchmark FB lacks. |
| Jegadeesh (1990), JF 45(3):881–898; Lehmann (1990), *Fads, Martingales, and Market Efficiency*, QJE 105(1):1–28 | Short-horizon (1-week to 1-month) return reversal | US stocks, 1960s–80s; weekly and monthly | Significant contrarian profits at one-week to one-month horizons. Lehmann reports that they persist after bid-ask and cost corrections. Lo–MacKinlay (1990, secondary) attribute much of this to lead-lag effects. | **Yes.** reversal16, l1 and l2_5 are this family. | Decades-later large caps. Factor-neutral evaluation. | The audit's lag check (A2) is the modern counterpart: l1 keeps 51% at next close and 89% at next open, and reversal16 keeps 98% at next close. Microstructure alone does not explain the small reversal IC, but it is small. |
| Blitz, Huij, Lansdorp & Verbeek (2013), *Short-term residual reversal*, J. Financial Markets 16(3):477–504 | Reversal ranked on **residual** returns (factor-model residuals, scaled by residual volatility) vs raw reversal | US stocks, including large caps post-1990; monthly | Residual reversal earns roughly twice the risk-adjusted return of conventional reversal and is profitable net of costs among large caps (abstract). | **No. This is a genuinely untested direction.** FB residualised the *training target* but never the *signal*: reversal16 and the lag blocks rank raw past returns. | Monthly; Fama–French residuals over 36 months. | A **residual-reversal scorer** (past 5–20-day returns residualised on beta/sector/style at t, scaled by residual volatility). It is zero-parameter and cheap, and A2 shows the residual-target gain is mostly tilt removal, which is the same mechanism. |
| Blitz, Huij & Martens (2011), *Residual momentum*, J. Empirical Finance 18(3):506–521 | Momentum on residual returns | US stocks; 12-1 month | About twice the risk-adjusted profit of total-return momentum, and more stable | No (FB's l21_60 and ret_60 are raw). | Monthly. | A secondary member of the same residual-signal family. |
| Da, Liu & Schaumburg (2014), *A Closer Look at the Short-Term Return Reversal*, Management Science 60(3):658–674 | Decomposition of reversal profits | US stocks 1982–2009 | Only the within-industry, non-fundamental residual component reverses. A strategy on it earns several times standard reversal (3× in the working paper, 4× in the published abstract). | No. | Uses analyst-revision cash-flow news, which HyperShift lacks. | Supports a within-sector residual reversal scorer. Cash-flow-news filtering needs data the project does not have. |
| Nagel (2012), *Evaporating Liquidity*, RFS 25(7):2005–2039 | Reversal returns as compensation for liquidity provision | US stocks | Expected reversal returns are strongly time-varying and predictable by VIX, peaking in crises | Partly. FB's folds include 2020, where reversal-type arms do best (l1 +0.032, residual LightGBM +0.024). | — | Regime dependence is a plausible reason fold effects vary. A VIX-conditioned reversal test is a **hypothesis**, not a recommendation: one crisis year gives no power. |
| Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016), *A Taxonomy of Anomalies and Their Trading Costs*, RFS 29(1):104–147 | Net-of-cost profitability of anomalies | US stocks | Anomalies with turnover above about 50%/month rarely survive costs. A **buy/hold spread** (buffer) is the most effective simple cost mitigation. | Partly. Rule E is a long-only buffer; the market-neutral rules B and D had **no buffer**. | — | A3 shows neutral books are gross-positive but turn over 39–77% of NAV **per day**. The literature-backed fix to test is a buffered neutral book, not a new model. |
| Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), *…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns*, RFS 29(1):5–68 | Multiple testing in factor discovery | 316 published factors | A new factor needs t > 3.0, not 2.0 | Applies to HyperShift's adaptive history of more than 2,000 runs. | — | The audit's selection-adjusted numbers (A3: SPA p 0.040 vs 0, LOFO +0.0098) should be read against a t ≈ 3 hurdle. The residual model's t vs 0 is about 2.5 (0.0127/0.0051). |
| White (2000), *A Reality Check for Data Snooping*, Econometrica 68(5):1097–1126; Hansen (2005), *A Test for Superior Predictive Ability*, JBES 23:365–380 | Testing the best of many models against a benchmark | — | Bootstrap max-statistic tests; SPA is less conservative when poor alternatives are included | Not used in FB; applied in this audit (A3). | — | Use SPA as the default "best-of-pool" test in future studies. |
| Bailey & López de Prado (2014), *The Deflated Sharpe Ratio*, JPM 40(5):94–107; Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017), *The Probability of Backtest Overfitting*, J. Computational Finance 20(4) | Selection-bias-corrected Sharpe; CSCV estimate of overfitting | — | Ignoring the number of trials overstates performance. CSCV estimates PBO. | Not used in FB; CSCV applied in A3 (PBO 0.05 on IC). | A low PBO here says only that **arm rankings** persist across time blocks (residual-like arms beat raw-target arms). It is not evidence of tradable skill. | Report PBO and the deflated statistic for any future frozen choice. |
| Grinold (1989), *The Fundamental Law of Active Management*, JPM (secondary sources only) | IR ≈ IC × √breadth | — | The law assumes independent bets and a constant IC. Michaud & Michaud (2005, secondary) criticise its use to justify large universes and frequent trading. | — | A1(c): 77–89% of daily RankIC variance is common across stocks (above the 1/√n floor), so bets are far from independent. | Do not size expectations with the √breadth formula. Use the empirical IC IR (about 0.0127/0.096 × √50 ≈ 0.9 annualised for 5-day non-overlapping use, before costs). |

**Graph-based prediction.** RSR (Feng et al.), STHAN-SR and THINK were examined by HyperShift's Phases 1–2 directly against their own code or reimplementations (`docs/phase2/REPORT_CLOSURE.md`). The audit adds no external claim beyond that.

## Untested directions that the literature supports and HyperShift has not tested

1. **Residual-return *signals*** (residual reversal, within-sector reversal scaled by residual volatility): Blitz et al. 2013; Da, Liu & Schaumburg 2014. FB changed the target only.
2. **Turnover-aware neutral portfolio construction** (a buy/hold buffer on the long-short book): Novy-Marx & Velikov 2016.
3. **A survivor-free universe and point-in-time membership**: Krauss et al. 2017 show this is feasible for the S&P 500.
4. **Point-in-time firm characteristics at a monthly horizon**: GKX 2020. This needs new data; see the roadmap.
